# Survivorship Bias

Survivorship bias is the logical error of concentrating on the things that survived some process and overlooking those that did not, typically because of their lack of visibility. The classic example is Abraham Wald's analysis of WWII bombers: the military wanted to add armor where returning planes had bullet holes. Wald pointed out that the planes that did not return were the ones that needed armor — they were hit in places the survivors were not. The survivors were not representative of the whole. They were the lucky ones.

This pattern is dangerous because the failures are invisible. You see the successful startups, the shipped projects, the engineers who made it. You do not see the thousands of failed startups, the abandoned projects, the engineers who burned out. When you study only the survivors, you extract patterns that may have nothing to do with success — they may be patterns of luck, or patterns of what happens to work in specific historical conditions that no longer apply. Advice from survivors is biased toward what they did, not what actually caused their success.

The counter-strategy is to ask "where are the failures?" before drawing conclusions from visible successes. When you study a successful company, also study the companies that tried the same thing and failed. When you learn from a successful project, also examine the projects that used the same approach and did not succeed. The difference between the survivors and the failures is usually more informative than the characteristics of the survivors alone.

## Warning Signs

- Your analysis of success only includes data from successful cases
- You cannot name any failures in the domain you are studying
- Your conclusions are based on "all the successful companies do X" without checking whether failed companies also did X
- You dismiss failures as "they did it wrong" without examining whether they did the same things you plan to do
- Your research sample is exclusively composed of survivors

## Counter-strategy

- Before drawing conclusions from successes, explicitly ask "where are the failures?" and try to find them
- When studying successful cases, also study comparable failures and look for differences
- When someone says "Company X did Y and succeeded," ask "did any company do Y and fail?"
- Maintain a database of failures, not just successes, in your domain
- When advice comes from a survivor, ask what they think they did differently — then verify with someone who tried and failed
