# 权威盲从 (Authority Blindness)

Authority blindness is accepting an authority figure's judgment as sufficient basis for a decision without independent verification. The authority may be an expert, a senior leader, a famous investor, or a bestselling author. Their expertise is real, but expertise is domain-specific and fallible. When a single authority becomes the sole input to a decision, the decision inherits all of that authority's blind spots, biases, and outdated information — without the safety net of cross-verification.

The pattern exploits a legitimate cognitive shortcut. We cannot personally verify everything, so we delegate trust to authorities. This is efficient when the stakes are low and the authority is qualified. But as stakes rise, the cost of a wrong authority increases faster than the cost of verification. A doctor's misdiagnosis, an analyst's wrong projection, a mentor's outdated advice — each can lead to catastrophic decisions if accepted without question. Cross-verification is not disrespect; it is diligence.

## How It Manifests

- "[Famous person] said to do it this way" as the primary justification
- Following a single analyst's recommendation without checking alternative analyses
- Adopting a strategy because a successful competitor uses it, without understanding why
- Making a medical or financial decision based solely on one professional's advice
- Challenging the authority feels inappropriate or disrespectful

## Warning Signs

- You cannot name a second independent source that agrees
- You feel uncomfortable questioning the authority — that discomfort is the pattern
- The authority's advice feels conveniently aligned with what you wanted to do anyway
- The authority's expertise is in a different domain than the decision
- No contrary opinion has been sought or considered

## Counter-strategy

- For significant decisions, require at least two independent sources that reach the same conclusion through different reasoning
- "Independent" means they did not cite each other — trace the chain of influence
- Actively seek the strongest case AGAINST the authority's position
- Ask the authority directly: "What would change your mind?" — if they can't answer, be cautious
- Distinguish expertise from infallibility — experts are right more often, not always

## Related Principles

- 能力圈 (Competence Circle): verify that the authority's expertise covers THIS specific question
- 逆向思维 (Inversion): what if the authority is wrong? What would that look like?
