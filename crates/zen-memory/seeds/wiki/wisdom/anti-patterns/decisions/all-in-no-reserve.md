# 孤注一掷 (All-In No Reserve)

Going all-in is committing more than 80% of available resources — capital, time, or attention — to a single course of action without a fallback plan. The appeal is focus: maximum resource allocation theoretically maximizes the probability of success. But it also maximizes the cost of failure. When you commit everything, you eliminate the ability to adapt, pivot, or recover. A single unexpected event converts a manageable setback into a terminal outcome.

Munger's principle of preserving optionality is the counterweight. Optionality — having multiple paths remaining open — has intrinsic value that is often invisible until it is needed. A decision that commits 60% of resources and preserves 40% for adaptation has a structurally better risk-adjusted return than one that commits 100%. The extra 40% is not "wasted" — it is insurance against the unknowable. The worst time to discover you need reserves is after you've spent them.

## How It Manifests

- Investing life savings into a single venture with no emergency fund
- Committing an entire quarter's engineering capacity to one feature
- Hiring exclusively for one role with no cross-training
- Signing a long-term lease that consumes all cash flow
- Burning bridges with current employer/clients before the new path is validated

## Warning Signs

- You cannot articulate what happens if this doesn't work
- The fallback plan is "it has to work" — that is not a plan
- More than 80% of capital, time, or attention is committed to one outcome
- Stress levels are high because failure is not survivable
- You are making decisions out of fear of losing the committed resources

## Counter-strategy

- Before committing, define: "If this fails, what is my next move?"
- If the answer is "there is no next move," you are about to go all-in — reconsider
- Cap maximum resource commitment at 70-80%, reserving the rest for adaptation
- Maintain a separate emergency fund that is never committed to any venture
- Define the fallback plan IN WRITING before committing — not after things go wrong
- Track optionality count: how many paths remain open from your current state?

## Related Principles

- 永远有筹码 (Keep Chips): the core principle — never bet everything
- 二阶思维 (Second-Order): what happens if you lose? Can you recover?
