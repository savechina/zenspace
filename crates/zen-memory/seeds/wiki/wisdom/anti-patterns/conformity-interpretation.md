# Conformity Interpretation

Conformity interpretation is the tendency to read data in a way that confirms what you already believe. The data does not change — your interpretation of it does. You see what you expect to see. When metrics are ambiguous, you choose the reading that supports your existing plan. When user behavior is unclear, you interpret it in the most favorable light. The result is a growing gap between your model of reality and reality itself.

This pattern is especially dangerous because it masquerades as data-driven decision making. You can point to the same chart that everyone else can see. But your interpretation has been filtered through your assumptions, and you present that filtered reading as if it were the data itself. The team makes decisions based on your interpretation, not the raw data. Over time, the organization builds a shared narrative that diverges from actual user behavior. By the time the divergence becomes visible, it has been compounding for months.

The counter-strategy is adversarial interpretation. Before analyzing data, write down your hypothesis. Then explicitly look for evidence that contradicts it. If you cannot find any, you have either confirmed your hypothesis or you have not looked hard enough — and the latter is more likely. Assign someone on the team to argue the opposite interpretation. The exercise is not about being contrarian. It is about forcing yourself to see what the data actually says rather than what you want it to say.

## Warning Signs

- Your interpretation of data always supports the plan you already wanted to execute
- You cannot recall the last time data changed your mind about a significant decision
- You frame ambiguous data in the most favorable light without acknowledging the ambiguity
- Team members who disagree with your interpretation stop speaking up
- You selectively present data that supports your position while omitting contradictory signals

## Counter-strategy

- Write your hypothesis before looking at the data; then search for disconfirming evidence
- When presenting data to the team, present the strongest counter-interpretation alongside your own
- Ask a colleague to independently interpret the same data before sharing your reading
- Maintain a log of predictions and outcomes to calibrate your interpretive accuracy
- When data is ambiguous, acknowledge the ambiguity rather than forcing a narrative
