# Data Worship

Data worship is the belief that if something cannot be measured, it does not matter. It manifests as an exclusive reliance on quantitative metrics while dismissing qualitative insights, user narratives, and professional judgment. The worshipper says "show me the numbers" as if numbers alone constitute truth. But numbers are summaries. They compress complex realities into single values, and in that compression, they lose context, nuance, and meaning.

This pattern is dangerous because it creates a false sense of objectivity. A metric feels more objective than a hunch, so the metric wins. But the metric may be measuring the wrong thing, measuring it poorly, or missing the dimension that actually matters. A/B testing can tell you which headline gets more clicks. It cannot tell you whether the clicks represent genuine interest or clickbait manipulation. Metrics tell you what happened. They rarely tell you why. And "why" is where the useful decisions live.

The counter-strategy is structured complementarity. Use data to identify patterns and generate hypotheses. Use qualitative methods — interviews, observation, open-ended feedback — to understand why those patterns exist. Neither alone is sufficient. The best decisions come from triangulation: data tells you where to look, and human judgment tells you what you are seeing. When someone says "the data says X," the correct follow-up is always "and what does the data not say?"

## Warning Signs

- You dismiss user complaints because the metrics look good
- You cannot explain why a metric moved, only that it did
- You optimize for the metric rather than the outcome the metric is supposed to represent
- Qualitative feedback is treated as anecdotal and therefore less valid than quantitative data
- You feel uncomfortable making decisions without a chart, even when your judgment is clear

## Counter-strategy

- Pair every quantitative metric with at least one qualitative insight source
- When a metric improves but you suspect the improvement is not real, investigate qualitatively
- Ask "what would have to be true for this metric to be misleading?" and check those conditions
- Schedule regular qualitative reviews (user interviews, observation sessions) alongside data reviews
- When presenting a data-driven recommendation, explicitly state the assumptions and limitations of the data
