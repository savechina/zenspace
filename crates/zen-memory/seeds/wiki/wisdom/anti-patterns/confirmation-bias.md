# Confirmation Bias

Confirmation bias is the tendency to search for, interpret, and remember information in a way that confirms your existing beliefs. It is perhaps the most pervasive cognitive bias because it operates below conscious awareness. You do not choose to seek confirming evidence — your brain does it automatically. The search terms you use, the articles you click on, the people you ask, the data you examine — all are unconsciously filtered to support what you already believe.

This pattern is dangerous because it creates an illusion of evidence. You feel well-informed because you have gathered many pieces of information that support your position. But you have been shopping in a store that only sells what you already have. The information is real. The completeness of your picture is not. Confirmation bias does not add false information — it subtracts disconfirming information, which is far harder to detect. You do not notice what you did not look for.

The counter-strategy is deliberate disconfirmation. Before committing to a decision, explicitly ask: "What evidence would change my mind?" If you cannot answer that question, you are not reasoning — you are rationalizing. Then go find that evidence. Assign someone on the team to argue the opposite position. Read the strongest critique of your plan, not the weakest. The goal is not to destroy your confidence but to ensure your confidence is based on the full picture, not the comfortable part of it.

## Warning Signs

- You can quickly list five pieces of evidence supporting your position but struggle to list any against it
- Your research process consistently leads you to confirming information
- You dismiss contradictory evidence as "biased" while accepting confirming evidence uncritically
- You feel defensive when someone challenges your position rather than curious
- Your beliefs have not changed despite exposure to new information

## Counter-strategy

- Before a decision, write down what evidence would change your mind — then go find it
- Ask someone you disagree with to present the strongest case against your position
- When you find confirming evidence, actively search for disconfirming evidence of equal quality
- Track your predictions and compare them with outcomes to calibrate your belief accuracy
- When you notice yourself dismissing contradictory evidence, ask "what if this is true?"
