# Anchoring Effect

The anchoring effect is the tendency to rely too heavily on the first piece of information encountered when making decisions. The first number you hear becomes the reference point around which all subsequent estimates cluster. If someone says "this project will take six months," your estimate for the same project will tend to be near six months — even if six months is wildly wrong. The anchor does not have to be correct to be influential. It just has to come first.

This pattern is dangerous because the anchor operates even when you know it is arbitrary. In experiments, people asked to estimate a number after seeing a random number (generated by rolling dice) still anchored to the random number. The effect is unconscious and persistent. In practical settings, the anchor is rarely random — it comes from a colleague, a vendor, a previous project, or a document. But the fact that it came from somewhere does not make it accurate. It just makes it sticky.

The counter-strategy is to generate your own estimate before exposure to others' estimates. If you are in a meeting where someone will propose a timeline, write down your independent estimate first. Then compare. The gap between your estimate and theirs is where the conversation should focus. If you cannot avoid hearing the anchor first, explicitly ask yourself: "Would I have estimated this if I had not heard that number?" If the answer is no, you are anchored.

## Warning Signs

- Your estimates cluster tightly around numbers proposed by others
- You adjust your estimate in the direction of the first number you heard
- You cannot articulate why you chose a specific number apart from "it seemed about right"
- When the anchor is removed (e.g., a new person joins who does not know it), estimates shift significantly
- You feel uncomfortable proposing an estimate that is far from the anchor, even when the evidence supports it

## Counter-strategy

- Before hearing others' estimates, write down your own independent estimate
- When you notice an anchor, ask "what would I estimate if I had not heard this number?"
- Use multiple independent reference points rather than relying on a single anchor
- When presenting an estimate, provide a range rather than a point, and justify the range independently
- In planning sessions, have team members submit estimates independently before group discussion
