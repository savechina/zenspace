# First Principles Thinking

First principles thinking means breaking a problem down to its most basic, undeniably true elements and reasoning upward from there. Instead of asking "how is this usually done?" you ask "what do I actually know to be true, and what can I derive from it?" This is the difference between reasoning by analogy (what others do) and reasoning from foundations (what is actually the case).

The method is simple but requires discipline. When facing a problem, list the assumptions you are carrying. For each assumption, ask: "Is this demonstrably true, or is it something I inherited?" Many inherited assumptions are correct. But some are not, and the ones that are wrong can be the most expensive to keep. First principles thinking does not mean rejecting all precedent — it means understanding why precedent exists before following it.

Example: when Elon Musk wanted to reduce the cost of rockets, he did not ask "how do we make rockets cheaper?" He asked "what are rockets made of?" The answer was aerospace-grade aluminum alloys, titanium, copper, carbon fiber. He then asked "what do those materials cost on the commodity market?" The materials were roughly 2% of a typical rocket's price. The rest was process, bureaucracy, and legacy assumptions. By reasoning from first principles, he identified that the cost problem was not a materials problem but an engineering and manufacturing problem. SpaceX was built on that insight.

## When to Apply

- When a problem seems intractable using conventional approaches
- When inherited assumptions are blocking progress but nobody can articulate why they exist
- When you are designing something new and want to avoid inheriting legacy constraints unnecessarily
- When someone says "that's just how it's done" — that is the moment to dig deeper
- When cost, time, or complexity seems unreasonably high — question whether the constraints are real
