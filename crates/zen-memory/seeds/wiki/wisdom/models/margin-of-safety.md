# Margin of Safety — 永远有筹码

Margin of safety is a principle from value investing that applies far beyond finance: always keep a buffer between what you expect and what you need to survive. In investing, it means buying a security at a significant discount to its intrinsic value so that even if your analysis is wrong, you do not lose money. In life, it means never betting so much on a single outcome that failure would be catastrophic.

Warren Buffett built Berkshire Hathaway on this principle. He has never taken a leveraged bet that could wipe him out. He keeps enormous cash reserves — sometimes hundreds of billions — not because he lacks ideas, but because he insists on the ability to act when opportunities arise and to survive when they do not. The companies he buys have wide moats and strong balance sheets. His personal rule is simple: "Never risk what you have and need for what you don't have and don't need."

The principle is counterintuitive because it appears conservative in a world that celebrates boldness. But margin of safety is not about avoiding risk — it is about surviving long enough for your judgment to be proven right. A gambler who bets everything on one hand either wins big or is eliminated. An investor who keeps margin of safety may win smaller on each bet but never gets eliminated, and over time, compound returns dominate. Margin of safety is the structural advantage that turns survival into prosperity.

## When to Apply

- When the potential upside is large but the downside is catastrophic — margin of safety forces you to size the bet so the downside is merely uncomfortable
- When you are confident — that is precisely when margin of safety is most important, because confidence is often wrong
- When committing a large portion of resources to any single decision — ask "what happens if I am completely wrong?"
- When the environment is uncertain and the range of possible outcomes is wide — margin of safety is the only rational response to uncertainty
- When someone promises high returns with low risk — that is when margin of safety is most often absent

## Questions to Ask

- If this decision goes completely wrong, am I still here to make the next decision?
- What buffer exists between my expectations and my survival threshold?
- Am I sizing this bet based on my confidence or based on my ability to withstand being wrong?
- Is there a way to get most of the upside with significantly less downside?
