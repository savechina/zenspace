# Inversion

Inversion is the practice of approaching a problem backwards. Instead of asking "how do I succeed?" ask "what would guarantee failure?" Then avoid those things. This inversion often produces more actionable insight than direct reasoning because failure modes are usually clearer and more numerous than success paths. It is easier to list the things that will kill a project than to list the things that will make it great.

Charlie Munger uses inversion as a primary thinking tool. When asked how to have a good marriage, he does not list qualities of good marriages. He lists the ways marriages fail: contempt, dishonesty, laziness, selfishness. Avoid those, and you are well ahead of most people. The same logic applies to projects, decisions, and systems. Identify the guaranteed failure modes and eliminate them. What remains is a much higher probability of success.

The mechanical application: write down the goal, then write "what would absolutely guarantee that this goal fails?" List at least five things. For each one, ask "am I currently doing any of these?" The answers are often uncomfortable and always useful. This is especially powerful when the goal feels vague or the path unclear — failure modes are concrete even when success is abstract.

## When to Apply

- When you are unsure how to succeed — list the ways to fail and eliminate them
- When a project feels risky but you cannot articulate why — inversion exposes the specific risks
- When planning a complex initiative — identify the three things that would kill it and protect against them
- When someone proposes an ambitious plan — ask "what would make this fail?" before asking "what would make it succeed?"
- When you notice a pattern of repeated failures in a domain — the failure modes are probably consistent; find and address them
