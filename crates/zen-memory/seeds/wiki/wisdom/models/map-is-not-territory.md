# The Map Is Not the Territory

Every mental model, plan, or representation of reality is an abstraction — a map. The map is useful precisely because it simplifies, but that simplification means it always omits something. Korzybski's insight is not merely philosophical: it is operational. When you mistake the model for the thing itself, you make decisions based on details that were deliberately left out.

The danger compounds in domains where the map has become authoritative. Budgets, schedules, requirements documents, and architecture diagrams all start as maps. Teams begin optimizing for the map rather than the territory it represents. A project plan that is 90% complete may have no usable output. A spec that looks elegant on paper may be impossible to implement. The map has become the thing people serve.

Concrete example: a team builds a feature based on a user persona document. The persona says users want "advanced filtering." The team spends three months building it. The feature ships and nobody uses it. The persona was a map — a useful but imperfect model of who the user is. The team never went back to the territory to ask whether the map was still accurate. They served the document, not the user.

## When to Apply

- When making decisions based on forecasts, projections, or models — ask what the model leaves out
- When a plan or spec feels too clean or certain — reality is messier than any document
- When you catch yourself defending a position using only data from one source
- When someone says "the data says..." — ask whether the data captures what actually matters
- When you notice stakeholders arguing about the map (the document) rather than the territory (the user's actual problem)
