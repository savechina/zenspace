# Second-Order Thinking

First-order thinking solves the immediate problem. Second-order thinking asks what happens as a result of that solution. Most people stop at the first order: "This is broken, so I will fix it this way." Second-order thinkers ask: "And then what? What does my fix break? Who is affected? What are the second, third, and fourth consequences?" This is not pessimism. It is the recognition that every action has ripple effects.

Howard Marks frames it this way: first-order thinking is easy and Everyone does it. Second-order thinking is hard because it requires you to think against the grain — to consider that the obvious solution might have hidden costs. A first-order thinker says "let's hire more engineers to ship faster." A second-order thinker asks: "Will more engineers actually ship faster, or will they increase coordination overhead? What happens to code quality? What does this signal to the existing team about their capacity?"

The discipline is mechanical: after every significant decision, write down or verbalize "and then what?" at least three times. The first "and then what?" reveals the obvious consequence. The second reveals the consequence of that consequence. The third reveals the systemic effect. Most expensive mistakes live at the second or third order, not the first.

## When to Apply

- When a solution seems obvious and clean — obvious solutions often have hidden second-order effects
- When proposing a change that affects multiple teams — ask what each team does differently as a result
- When you are tempted to optimize for one metric — ask what happens to other metrics as a side effect
- When reviewing a plan that only describes positive outcomes — force the "and then what?" conversation
- When a decision feels urgent — second-order thinking is the discipline that prevents expensive shortcuts
