# Hanlon's Razor

Hanlon's Razor advises: never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity. The "stupidity" here is not an insult — it refers to ignorance, forgetfulness, carelessness, miscommunication, or simple oversight. Most negative interactions are not attacks. They are mistakes. Assuming malice where there is none wastes your energy and damages relationships.

The razor is a social survival tool. When someone sends a harsh email, when a teammate misses a deadline, when a collaborator makes a change that breaks your work — the first assumption should be that they did not intend harm. This is not naivety. It is calibration. The base rate of intentional harm in professional settings is very low. The base rate of mistakes, miscommunication, and poor judgment is very high. Treating every mistake as a personal attack is a fast path to paranoia and burnout.

The practical discipline is a three-step pause. First, notice the negative reaction. Second, ask: "Is there a simpler explanation than malice?" Third, if there is, act on that explanation first. If the simpler explanation does not hold — if the pattern repeats, if the context makes malice likely — then reconsider. But start with the assumption of incompetence or oversight. It is usually right, and even when it is wrong, it gives you more room to respond strategically rather than reactively.

## When to Apply

- When a colleague does something that feels hostile — assume oversight first
- When you receive a critical comment that seems unfair — assume the critic has context you do not, not that they are attacking you
- When a process fails and someone is blaming you — assume the process is broken, not that the blamer is malicious
- When you notice yourself assuming the worst about someone's intent — check whether the simpler explanation fits
- When a conflict escalates — often the escalation is fueled by mutual attribution of malice that does not exist
