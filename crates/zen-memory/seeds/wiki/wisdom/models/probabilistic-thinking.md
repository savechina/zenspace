# Probabilistic Thinking

Probabilistic thinking means expressing uncertainty as degrees of belief rather than binary certainties. Instead of "this will work" or "this will fail," you say "I think there is a 70% chance this works, given what I know." This is not hedging — it is precision. Binary thinking is almost always wrong because reality rarely deals in absolutes. Probabilistic thinking forces you to be honest about what you know and what you do not.

The practical foundation is Bayesian updating: start with a prior belief, encounter new evidence, and update accordingly. If you believe a project has a 60% chance of success and then discover a critical dependency is unreliable, you update downward. If you then learn the dependency team has added redundancy, you update back up. The discipline is in the updating — most people form an initial belief and then defend it against evidence rather than incorporating it.

Annie Duke's framing is useful: treat every decision as a bet. When you say "I think X will happen," ask yourself "at what odds would I bet on this?" If you would not take the bet at even odds, your confidence is lower than your words suggest. This test exposes the gap between stated confidence and actual confidence, which is where many poor decisions originate.

## When to Apply

- When you catch yourself thinking in absolutes ("always," "never," "definitely") — translate to probabilities
- When evaluating competing plans — assign rough probabilities to each plan's success and compare expected values
- When new information arrives that challenges your plan — explicitly update your probability estimate rather than ignoring it
- When someone asks "will this work?" — answer with a probability range, not a yes/no
- When reviewing past decisions — compare your stated confidence at the time with the actual outcome to calibrate future estimates
